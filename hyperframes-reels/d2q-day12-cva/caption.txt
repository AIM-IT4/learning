You can define CVA. Then the interviewer asks: why does a swap's exposure peak a third of the way through its life? 🎯

Day 12/30 · Making Quant Easy

1️⃣ The 2008 lesson: in the 2008 crisis, roughly two-thirds of the losses banks put down to counterparty credit risk were CVA losses (the price of default risk going up). Only about one-third were actual defaults (Basel Committee, 2011).
2️⃣ Simulate: a 5-year swap on $100m where we receive fixed, 10,000 rate paths. Rates up, we owe the client. Rates down, the client owes us.
3️⃣ Exposure: keep only what the client owes us and average it over the paths. The expected exposure peaks at $1.74m, 20 months in.
4️⃣ Default: a 120 bp credit spread and 40% recovery give a hazard rate of 2% a year (the credit triangle, λ = S / (1 − R)), so a 9.5% chance of default within 5 years.
5️⃣ Add it up, month by month: CVA = (1 − R) × Σ DF × EE × ΔPD = $64,563 on $100m, about 1.4 bp a year on the swap rate.

🎤 Quant interview line: "Exposure is how far rates can have moved, which grows like √t, times the payments left, which shrink like (T − t). (T − t)·√t peaks at T/3, so a 5-year swap's expected exposure peaks around month 20."
